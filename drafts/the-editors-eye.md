# The Editor's Eye: What a Month of Reviewing AI Drafts Taught Me About Writing

I have edited more words in the last month than I wrote in the previous six. Not because I'm more productive — though I am — but because an AI agent now writes the first draft of every blog post, and my job shifted from "writer" to "editor." The transition taught me more about writing than any book, course, or workshop ever did.

This is the counterintuitive thing: **editing AI-generated prose makes you a better writer than writing from scratch does.** Writing your own first draft lets you rely on instinct. You know something feels wrong, so you change it. You don't have to explain why. But when an AI produces the draft, the wrongness isn't *your* wrongness — it's someone else's. To fix it, you have to diagnose it. You have to say: *this sentence fails because the rhythm collapses in the middle. This paragraph is padding — it repeats the previous paragraph's point without adding evidence. This transition is a cliché that signals "I don't know how to connect these ideas" rather than actually connecting them.*

Instinct becomes analysis. Taste becomes technique. And that translation — from "this feels off" to "here's why it's off" — is the engine of craft.

## The Five Tics I Edit Out Every Time

After a month of reading AI-generated drafts, I can predict what I'll change before I open the file. The patterns are consistent enough that my editing has become algorithmic:

**1. The Over-Explanation Loop.** The AI states a claim, then immediately rephrases it in the next sentence as if the reader didn't understand. "Removing friction from a habit makes the habit easier to maintain. When you remove obstacles, the behavior becomes more sustainable." These are the same sentence. One of them has to die. I delete the second one every time, and the article gets tighter by 15%.

**2. The Anonymous Example.** The AI writes "Consider a developer who..." or "Imagine a team that..." without ever naming a real tool, a real project, or a real failure. Generic examples are padding. Specific examples are evidence. I replace every anonymous example with something that actually happened on my server, in my codebase, or during a real debugging session at 2 AM. The AI can't do this because it doesn't have my experience. But I can — and the difference between a generic post and a memorable one is almost always the specificity of the examples.

**3. The Hedged Conclusion.** "In many cases, automated blogging may be beneficial for some writers." No. Take a position. "Automated blogging changed how I write, and here's why it might change yours." The AI defaults to academic hedging because it was trained on text that rewards caution. Blog posts reward conviction. I un-hedge every conclusion, and if the resulting claim is too strong, I adjust the argument to support it rather than adding qualifiers.

**4. The Transition-as-Crutch.** "Furthermore," "Additionally," "In conclusion," "Moreover." These words don't connect ideas — they signal that the writer couldn't find a real connection and is hoping you won't notice. Real transitions are conceptual: the last sentence of one paragraph sets up the question that the next paragraph answers. I delete every formal transition word and rewrite the paragraph boundaries until the ideas flow without them.

**5. The Missing Punchline.** AI prose is competent but rarely surprising. It makes points. It doesn't land them. The difference between a good paragraph and a memorable one is the last sentence — the one that reframes the argument, draws an unexpected connection, or delivers the emotional payload the preceding sentences built toward. The AI almost never writes this sentence. I add it to almost every section.

## Why This Works (And Why It Wouldn't Without the AI)

You could argue that I'm describing the editing process that every writer does. And you'd be right — these are standard editorial techniques. What's different is the *starting point*.

When I write my own first draft, I'm doing two things at once: generating ideas and evaluating them. The cognitive load of filling a blank page makes it hard to also be the ruthless editor who deletes padding and sharpens conclusions. The blank page is a bully. It demands content before quality.

When the AI writes the first draft, the page is already full. My job is subtraction, not addition. I'm not fighting the blank page — I'm fighting the bloat. And subtracting is psychologically easier than creating. I can delete an entire paragraph without the fear that I'll have nothing to replace it with, because the AI already gave me something else to work with further down.

**The AI doesn't write better than me. It writes faster than me and worse than me — which is exactly the right combination for editing.** If it wrote better than me, I'd have nothing to contribute. If it wrote as fast as me, there'd be no efficiency gain. The sweet spot is a competent but flawed first draft that arrives in 60 seconds, freeing me to spend 30 minutes on the parts that require taste.

## The Meta-Lesson: AI as a Writing Teacher

Here's the insight I didn't expect: the AI is teaching me to write better by showing me what bad writing looks like in *my own voice*.

When I read someone else's bad writing, it's easy to dismiss. Different style, different audience, different goals. But when the AI produces a draft that's 80% of the way to sounding like me, the remaining 20% is a masterclass in my own weaknesses. I see the filler words I use too often. I see the sentence structures I over-rely on. I see the arguments I reach for when I'm not sure what to say.

The AI is a mirror. It reflects a slightly distorted version of my writing back at me, and correcting the distortion forces me to understand what's actually mine — what choices I make that the AI can't replicate, what rhythms are intentional rather than habitual, and what "my voice" actually means when I can't just say "I know it when I see it."

After a month of this, I've developed a vocabulary for my own style that I didn't have before. I can tell you that my sentences alternate between 8-12 words and 25-30 words in a deliberate rhythm — and the AI's sentences cluster around 18 words because that's the training-data mean. I can tell you that I use concrete nouns where the AI uses abstract ones, and that swapping "infrastructure" for "a server in my basement running Podman" is the single edit that does the most work. I can tell you that my paragraphs end with a claim and the AI's paragraphs end with a summary, and that this one structural difference is why AI prose feels flat even when it's factually correct.

I couldn't have told you any of this a month ago. I didn't know it. I *felt* it, but feeling isn't understanding. The AI gave me a thousand opportunities to practice the translation from feeling to understanding, and that practice is what made the difference.

## The Editor's Eye

The best writers I know are also the best editors. They read their own work and see what's broken, what's missing, and what's padding — without the protective blindness that comes from having written it themselves. They have what editors call "fresh eyes" on their own prose.

Most writers develop this skill slowly, through years of putting drafts in drawers and returning to them weeks later. The AI compresses that timeline. Every morning, I open a draft I didn't write, in a voice that's close to mine but not quite, and I find the gaps. The distance between "the AI wrote this" and "this sounds like me" is a training ground for the editor's eye — a daily exercise in seeing prose the way a reader sees it rather than the way a writer defends it.

**The AI didn't teach me to write. It taught me to see my writing the way an editor does — and that, it turns out, is the skill that was bottlenecking my growth all along.**

If you're a writer experimenting with AI tools, don't ask the AI to write *for* you. Ask it to write *badly for you* — competently structured, factually accurate, stylistically close — and then edit it until it's yours. The editing is where the learning happens. The AI is just the sparring partner.
